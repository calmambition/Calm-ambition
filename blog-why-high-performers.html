Why High Performers
Are the Last to
Admit Burnout

The traits that built your career are the same ones that keep burnout invisible. Why capable people are the last to name what is happening to them.

Priyanka · 12 May 2026 · 5 min read

Nobody worries about you. That is the first problem.

The work lands on time, so your manager sees someone who handles things. You have been tired for two years, so at home it stopped being news a while ago. Every signal coming back at you says the situation is under control, and the one person positioned to argue is the one with the most invested in not looking too hard.

You have already diagnosed the wrong thing

Almost everyone I work with arrives with the problem already identified: the workload, the new manager, the quarter that will ease off in March. They have thought about it carefully and they are usually wrong. Most of a first session goes on discovering that the thing they named is a symptom of the thing they have not.

You diagnose the part you can see. Workload is visible and countable. How you are holding the work is neither.

Perfectionism splits in two

The usual advice gets this backwards, so it is worth separating carefully.

Andrew Hill and Thomas Curran pooled 43 studies covering 9,838 people for a 2016 meta-analysis in Personality and Social Psychology Review. They split perfectionism into two parts. Perfectionistic strivings are the high standards themselves. Perfectionistic concerns are the fear of falling short of them: the self-criticism, the worry about how a shortfall will be read.

Strivings showed a small negative or non-significant relationship with burnout. Concerns showed a medium-to-large positive one. Inside the work domain the gap widened at both ends.

So the bar is not the issue. What costs you is what runs in your head when you think you might miss it.

That distinction matters, because you have spent years as the person who delivers. Capability stopped being a skill you have and became a description of who you are. Admitting you are struggling reads, somewhere underneath, as admitting you are not who you said you were. So you get more sophisticated instead: a better system, a long weekend, a quiet promise to slow down once this stretch ends.

You are also quietly comparing

Against the colleague who had the visible breakdown. Against yourself at twenty-nine, doing this on less sleep and enjoying it. You set the bar somewhere above your own experience and conclude you have not reached it.

"I'm still functioning."
"I just need to get through this stretch."

Being able to keep going tells you nothing about whether you should be. It is the reason this stays invisible as long as it does.

The work is getting done. None of it is registering

There is a second pattern underneath, and it turns up regardless of seniority. Ask what someone got through last week and you get a pause, then a list of what is still outstanding. The finished work has already stopped counting. It cleared the bar, so it vanished, and attention moved on before anything landed.

Do that for a few years and the only place your progress exists is on paper.

The obvious fix is to make it visible: keep the completed work in front of you instead of clearing it away. That tends to backfire. Seeing everything finished means seeing everything outstanding in the same glance, and the outstanding list wins. What helps is changing the state you start the day in, rather than the record of what you got through.

Rest runs into a limit

The standard advice is to rest more, which treats burnout as a fuel problem: the tank is empty, so fill it.

Rest helps. It hits a wall with people who do not believe they are allowed to need it. If stopping raises a question about your worth, a week off becomes a week of low-grade dread with better scenery.

What changing it involves

It starts with naming what you have been tolerating and calling normal. The standards you hold yourself to that you would never hand a colleague. The signs you clocked months ago and decided did not count.

Then it moves to what makes slowing down feel unsafe. The perfectionistic concerns Hill and Curran measured live here: what you believe about yourself if you miss the bar, and what you think that would prove. Change the routine and leave that alone, and the change holds for about six weeks.

I spent fifteen years inside KPMG, Deloitte and CIBC. I know the pace those places run at, and I know what it costs to keep matching it while leaving the underneath part alone.

Recognising yourself is the hard part

If parts of this landed badly, that is the useful signal.

You are good at spotting problems in your work. Spotting problems in how you work needs a different kind of conversation than the ones available to you on an ordinary Tuesday.

If you cannot tell whether what you are experiencing counts, the uncertainty is your answer. People who are fine do not spend time wondering.
