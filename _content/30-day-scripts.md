Owner: Priyanka. Market: Australia and New Zealand. Written 12 November 2026.

These are 30 new short-video scripts. They sit alongside the 20 posts in `content-plan.md` and don't repeat them. Every first-person line comes from the "Your story" list in that plan. Edit any line that doesn't sound like you.

## 1. What's working right now, and why

### How far to trust this

Search can't see view counts, and my session can't open TikTok, Instagram or LinkedIn. The findings below come from 2026 marketing guides, news coverage and Australian surveys. The guides are agency blogs that quote each other, and none is a platform disclosure. Treat every number as a starting guess and let your own analytics replace it.

### Hooks that keep showing up

| Hook pattern | Why it works | Your version |
|---|---|---|
| **"Stop doing X"** | Gives the viewer permission to drop something they already resent. | "Stop calling it tired." |
| **"What nobody tells you about..."** | Promises inside knowledge. Your Big 4 years make the promise believable. | "What nobody tells you about being the reliable one." |
| **POV** | Puts the viewer inside a moment they know. Guides say it suits Reels, where people watch alone with sound on. | "POV: it's Sunday at 4pm and the week has already landed on you." |
| **Open on the pain scenario** | One LinkedIn guide reports higher completion for videos that open on a specific painful moment. Unverified, but it matches how your best site copy reads. | "You took the two weeks. By Wednesday it was gone." |
| **A named term** | People search and share a label. "Quiet cracking" has the most 2026 coverage. "Functional freeze" was flagged as a TikTok term in early 2026. | Explain the term, then say what you'd call it. |
| **Lead with the result** | Viewers decide in the first second or two, so the point comes first and the setup second. | State the claim in sentence one. Never open with "Hi, I'm Priyanka". |

### Formats that keep showing up

- **Talking to camera, 30 to 60 seconds, captions on.** Most guides put LinkedIn's range at 30 to 90 seconds and say completion matters more than views.
- **Looping checklist.** A short list on screen that people rewatch and save. Days 2, 6 and 22 use it.
- **Stories the viewer recognises from their own life.** This is your strongest material and needs no trend.
- **Cut the ending.** No "thanks for watching". End on the CTA line and stop.

### What's specific to Australia

- **The right to disconnect.** Australia has had the law since 2024, and surveys this year say behaviour hasn't caught up. An ADP poll from June 2026 found 38% of working Australians get contacted outside their hours at least once a week, and 21% say they can't disconnect at all. An ELMO survey found 62% reply after hours to look committed or avoid judgement. Days 7 and 20 use these.
- **Check before posting:** I read these figures in news coverage, not the original reports. Find the ADP and ELMO releases and confirm the numbers before you say them on camera.

### Where I'd push back on "viral"

Reach from strangers in other countries does nothing for you. You can only work with people in Australia and New Zealand, and you need discovery calls. A video that 800 Sydney professionals watch to the end beats one that 80,000 teenagers swipe past. So these scripts borrow the hook shapes that travel, and keep your message: staying in the career and changing how you run it.

Trend terms carry a risk too. "Functional freeze" and "quiet cracking" are social media labels. Neither is a diagnosis. The scripts say so.

### How to run the 30 days

Thirty videos in thirty days is a sprint, and I'd only do it if filming has become easy. Two honest options:

1. **Sprint:** film in three batches of ten and post one a day. Keep the Monday and Friday text posts from the main plan.
2. **Bank:** post these at the plan's pace, two or three a week, after post 20. This lasts you about three months.

Either way, post natively to LinkedIn first, then the same file to TikTok and Reels.

## 2. The scripts

Each script runs 30 to 50 seconds. The first line is the hook: say it straight to the lens, and put the on-screen text up for the first two seconds. CTAs rotate between follow, comment, save, the check-in and the discovery call, so you never ask for the same thing twice in a row.

### Week 1: "This is you"

**Day 1. POV: Sunday, 4pm**
On-screen: POV: Sunday 4pm and the week has already landed on you

> It's Sunday at four in the afternoon, and the week has already landed on you.
>
> Nothing has happened yet. No email, no meeting. But your shoulders are up and your head is in Monday.
>
> That's your body running the alarm before the work has even arrived. It never got the all-clear from last week.
>
> If Sunday afternoons feel like this most weeks, you're not short of rest days. You're short of recovery.
>
> Follow, because I'm going to show you the difference.

**Day 2. Stop calling it tired**
On-screen: Stop calling it tired

> Stop calling it tired.
>
> Tired goes away. Check yours against three things.
>
> One. A full night's sleep, and you wake up flat.
> Two. A whole weekend, and Monday feels the same.
> Three. A proper holiday, and it's gone within the week.
>
> If rest isn't fixing it, the word you want isn't tired.
>
> Save this, and check it again in a fortnight.

**Day 3. The reliable one**
On-screen: What nobody tells you about being the reliable one

> Here's what nobody tells you about being the reliable one.
>
> The reward for coping is more to cope with. You deliver, so you get the next thing. And the next.
>
> And because your work never slips, nobody asks how you are. I know, because mine never slipped, and nobody asked. I didn't ask myself either.
>
> Reliable is a reputation. It tells you nothing about how much you have left.
>
> If you're the reliable one, comment "me" so I know who I'm talking to.

**Day 4. Quiet cracking**
On-screen: "Quiet cracking" explained in 40 seconds

> You've probably seen the phrase "quiet cracking".
>
> It's a workplace label for people who keep showing up and keep delivering while their energy and morale wear down underneath.
>
> It's not a diagnosis. It's a name for something a lot of professionals already recognise.
>
> I'd describe it more plainly. You're performing well, and you're running on empty.
>
> The risk is that everyone, including you, reads the performance and misses the rest.
>
> Follow for what to do about it while you're still in the job.

**Day 5. Third person**
On-screen: I watched myself live my own life

> For years I watched myself live my own life.
>
> I was in every meeting. I said the right things. I hit the deadlines. And it felt like it was happening to someone else.
>
> I called it being busy. It was autopilot.
>
> Burnout researchers talk about "mental distance": pulling back from your work without ever deciding to.
>
> If your week feels like something you're watching, pay attention to that. And if the feeling is constant or frightening, talk to your GP.
>
> Follow if you've felt this.

**Day 6. Three questions**
On-screen: 3 questions before Monday

> Three questions before Monday.
>
> One. When did a weekend last leave you rested?
> Two. Can you switch off at night, or does work come to bed with you?
> Three. Are the people you love getting your energy, or what's left of it?
>
> If you didn't like your answers, I built a four-minute check-in that looks at your body, mind, drive and life, and gives you three small things to try.
>
> The link's in my profile.

**Day 7. The 7pm "quick call"**
On-screen: Australia has a right to disconnect. Are you using it?

> Australia has had a right to disconnect since 2024. So why are you still answering at nine at night?
>
> One poll this year found more than a third of working Australians get contacted outside their hours every week. One in five said they can't disconnect at all.
>
> A law can give you the right. It can't build the habit.
>
> Start with one evening this week. Pick a finish time, put it in your calendar like a client meeting, and close the laptop.
>
> Which evening are you picking? Tell me in the comments.

### Week 2: "Here's why"

**Day 8. Rest isn't recovery**
On-screen: You rested all weekend. Why are you still exhausted?

> You rested all weekend. So why are you still exhausted?
>
> Because rest means you've stopped working. Recovery means your body has come back down.
>
> You can lie on the couch for six hours with your mind still in the office. That's rest with the alarm still on.
>
> Recovery needs a signal that the demand has passed. A hard finish. A walk without your phone. An evening that belongs to someone you love.
>
> Follow, and I'll give you one of those to try tomorrow.

**Day 9. Why the holiday wears off**
On-screen: Your holiday wore off in a week. That's normal.

> Your holiday wore off within a week, and research says that's normal.
>
> Studies that track people through their holidays keep finding the same thing. Wellbeing goes up while you're away, and it's back where it started within the first week at work.
>
> Longer holidays didn't change that.
>
> So the fix isn't a bigger break. It's what an ordinary Tuesday looks like when you get back.
>
> How long did your last holiday last? Comment the number of days.

**Day 10. One hard finish**
On-screen: Try this: one hard finish a week

> If every workday bleeds into the evening, try this.
>
> Pick one evening this week. Give it a finish time. Put it in your calendar the way you'd book a client.
>
> When the time comes, close the laptop and leave it closed.
>
> One evening. That's the whole experiment. Run it for two weeks and watch what happens to that night's sleep.
>
> Save this, and book the evening before you scroll on.

**Day 11. Functional freeze**
On-screen: "Functional freeze": what it is and isn't

> There's a term going around called "functional freeze".
>
> People use it for this: you get through the whole workday, and then you get home and can't do anything. Not the dishes, not the message you owe a friend.
>
> It's a social media label. It isn't a diagnosis.
>
> What I'd say is this. If work takes everything you have, there's nothing left for the rest of your life, and that's worth taking seriously.
>
> If it's been going on for weeks, see your GP. And follow for small ways to keep something back.

**Day 12. Stop trying to stay calm**
On-screen: Stop trying to stay calm under pressure

> Stop trying to stay calm under pressure.
>
> A 2026 study put people through the same stress test four times. The resilient ones didn't react less. They recovered faster after each round.
>
> It was a small study of young men, so hold it lightly. But it matches what I see in high performers.
>
> The goal isn't feeling nothing in the hard meeting. It's how quickly you come back down afterwards.
>
> So ask yourself a new question. After a hard day, how long until you feel normal again?
>
> Tell me your answer in the comments.

**Day 13. Busy season**
On-screen: Busy season is supposed to end

> Busy season is supposed to end.
>
> That's the deal. You push for a stretch, then the stretch finishes and you get your breath back.
>
> I spent fifteen years in corporate telling myself it would ease once the current stretch was over. There was always another stretch.
>
> If you can't name the last time work let up, you're not in a busy season. This is how your year runs now.
>
> When did your last one end? Tell me below.

**Day 14. Inside the check-in**
On-screen: 4 minutes. 14 questions. 3 things to try.

> This is the check-in I built for people who are still performing and running on empty.
>
> Fourteen questions, about four minutes. It looks at four areas: your body, your mind, your drive, and your life outside work.
>
> You get a readout in plain language and three small things to try over the next two weeks.
>
> It's a self-reflection tool, not a diagnosis.
>
> The link is in my profile.

### Week 3: "Here's what to try"

**Day 15. What I'd tell myself at year ten**
On-screen: What I'd tell myself at year 10 of corporate

> If I could talk to myself ten years into corporate, I'd say three things.
>
> Your work being immaculate doesn't mean you're okay.
>
> "Once this stretch is over" is not a plan. There's always another stretch.
>
> And two weeks off won't fix it, because the workload was never the whole problem. It's how you've been running the entire time.
>
> I stopped later than I should have. You don't have to.
>
> Follow if you needed one of those today.

**Day 16. The advice you can't take**
On-screen: You'd know exactly what to tell a colleague

> If a colleague described your week to you, you'd know exactly what to tell them.
>
> Slow down. Hand that off. Take the leave.
>
> You just can't do it yourself.
>
> That gap isn't a lack of knowledge. You already know. When you're depleted, doing the new thing is the hard part, so you fall back on the old routine.
>
> So start smaller than your own advice. One change you could manage on your worst day.
>
> What would you tell a colleague in your position? Write it in the comments.

**Day 17. "After this project"**
On-screen: "After this project" is not a plan

> "After this project, I'll slow down."
>
> I said a version of that for years. The stretch ended, and another one started the same week.
>
> If rest only exists in the future, it never arrives.
>
> So put something in this week, while the project is still on. Ten minutes outside between meetings. One evening with a finish time.
>
> Small, and inside the busy week. That's the only kind that lasts.
>
> Save this for the next time you hear yourself say it.

**Day 18. Ten minutes, no phone**
On-screen: Try this: 10 minutes outside, no phone

> Try this tomorrow between two meetings.
>
> Ten minutes outside. No phone, no podcast. Just walking and daylight.
>
> The no-phone part matters most. If you take your inbox with you, you've only moved the work outside.
>
> You're giving your body ten minutes with nothing asked of it. For most busy professionals, that's rare.
>
> Do it daily for two weeks and notice your afternoons.
>
> Save this, and put it in tomorrow's calendar now.

**Day 19. Leftovers**
On-screen: Who gets what's left of you?

> Work gets the best of you. Who gets what's left?
>
> You're home, and you nod at the people who matter while your mind is still at the office.
>
> They're getting leftovers. They can tell.
>
> Try one evening this week that belongs to someone you care about, with your phone in another room. Not on silent. In another room.
>
> One evening won't fix everything. It shows you how hard it has become to be present, and that's useful to know.
>
> Who's your evening for? Tell me below.

**Day 20. Replying to look committed**
On-screen: Why you reply at 10pm

> Be honest about the ten o'clock reply.
>
> An Australian survey this year found most workers who answer after hours do it to look committed, or to avoid being judged. The work itself could have waited.
>
> You're paying with your evening to manage an impression.
>
> Try this. The next message that can wait until morning, let it wait until morning. Once. Then see whether anything happens.
>
> Usually nothing does.
>
> Follow for more ways to get your evenings back.

Note: confirm the ELMO figure (62%) before filming.

**Day 21. Cynicism**
On-screen: You're not becoming a cynical person

> If you've turned cynical about work you used to care about, you haven't changed as a person.
>
> Burnout researchers treat that pulling away as a core sign. They call it mental distance. You withdraw from the work to protect what you have left.
>
> So the eye-roll in the team meeting is information.
>
> Try protecting thirty minutes this week for the one part of your role you still care about.
>
> Which part is that for you? Comment it. Naming it is the first step.

**Day 23. The worry window**
On-screen: Try this: give the worry an appointment

> If one worry keeps looping all day, give it an appointment.
>
> Pick fifteen minutes later today. Say, five o'clock. That's when you'll think about it on purpose.
>
> When it turns up before then, tell it: five o'clock.
>
> You're not ignoring the worry. You're choosing when it gets your attention.
>
> Try it for two weeks.
>
> Save this for the next time your mind won't let something go.
