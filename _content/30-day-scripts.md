## 1. What's working right now, and why

### How far to trust this

Search can't see view counts, and this session can't open TikTok, Instagram or LinkedIn. The hook and format findings come from 2026 marketing guides that quote each other. None is a platform disclosure. Treat the numbers as starting guesses and let your own analytics replace them.

### Hooks that keep showing up

| Hook pattern | Why it works | Example from the scripts |
|---|---|---|
| **"Stop doing X"** | Gives permission to drop something the viewer already resents. | Day 2: "Stop calling it tired." |
| **"What nobody tells you about..."** | Promises inside knowledge. Your Big 4 years make it believable. | Day 3: "What nobody tells you about being the reliable one." |
| **POV** | Puts the viewer inside a moment they know. Suits Reels, where people watch alone. | Day 1: "POV: it's Sunday at 4pm." |
| **Open on the painful moment** | One LinkedIn guide reports higher completion for videos that start on a specific pain scenario. | Day 9: "You took two weeks off. By Wednesday it was gone." |
| **A named term** | People search and share labels. "Quiet cracking" has the most 2026 coverage. "Functional freeze" was flagged as a TikTok term this year. | Days 4 and 11 |
| **Lead with the result** | Viewers decide in the first second or two. The point goes first. | Every script |

### What makes the middle worth watching

The first version of these scripts mostly described the feeling. The top accounts in the niche do three more things in the middle, and every script below now does them:

1. **Explain why it happens.** One mechanism in plain words, with a named study.
2. **Give a tool with steps.** Something specific enough to try tonight.
3. **Say what to expect.** How long, what changes, and what doesn't.

### Formats

- **Talking to camera, 60 to 90 seconds, captions on.** LinkedIn guides put the range at 30 to 90 seconds and say people watching to the end matters more than views. If a script runs long in your mouth, cut the "why" paragraph to one sentence before you cut the tool.
- **On-screen lists** for the step-by-step parts. People pause and save them.
- **End on the CTA.** No "thanks for watching".

### Australia-specific

- **The right to disconnect.** Surveys this year say behaviour hasn't caught up with the law. An ADP poll from June 2026 found 38% of working Australians are contacted outside their hours at least once a week, and 21% say they can't disconnect at all. An ELMO survey found 62% reply after hours to look committed or avoid judgement. I read these in news coverage, not the original reports. Confirm them before filming days 7 and 20.

### Where I'd push back

Views from other countries don't book calls. You can only take clients in Australia and New Zealand. A video 800 Sydney professionals finish beats one 80,000 strangers swipe past. And trend terms like "quiet cracking" and "functional freeze" are social media labels, not diagnoses. The scripts say so every time.

### How to run them

You haven't posted video one yet, so I'd treat these as a bank: two or three a week after post 20. If filming turns out to be easy, run them daily in three batches of ten. Post natively to LinkedIn first, then the same file to TikTok and Reels.

## 2. Your intro line

### Where it goes

Put it second, never first. The hook comes first (seconds 0 to 2), then the intro line (seconds 2 to 6), then the "Why". If you open with who you are, people swipe before they hear why they should care. Every guide on hooks says the same thing: lead with the viewer's problem.

So every video runs: **Hook → intro line → Why → Try this → CTA.**

### The line

Pick one and say it the same way in every video, so it becomes familiar.

**Option A (recommended):**
> I spent fifteen years in Big 4 and banking, and I burnt out without anyone noticing. Now I help professionals recover without giving up their careers.

**Option B (shorter, for fast-paced videos):**
> Fifteen years at KPMG, Deloitte and CIBC. Now I coach professionals through burnout.

**Option C (for story videos, days 5, 13, 25 and 28):**
> I burnt out after fifteen years in corporate, and I'm the reason I know what this looks like from the inside.

Option A does three jobs in about six seconds. "Big 4 and banking" tells your buyer you've worked in their world. "Without anyone noticing" names the exact thing they're living through, so it keeps them watching. "Without giving up their careers" tells them you won't tell them to quit, which is what most burnout content online says.

### The on-screen text

While you say it, put a small caption at the bottom of the screen (a lower third) that stays the same in every video:

> **Priyanka · Burnout recovery coach · Ex-KPMG, Deloitte, CIBC**

### Keep them watching

Add one line straight after the intro that promises something later in the video. Change it to fit the script:

> "Stay to the end for the exact line I'd use." (day 27)
> "The fix takes five minutes, and it's at the end." (day 10)
> "There's a study on this that surprised me." (days 9, 12, 15)

Only promise what the video delivers. A broken promise costs you more viewers next time than it gains this time.

### Check one fact

The scripts and your site say you burnt out at the end of your corporate years. If "I burnt out without anyone noticing" isn't how you'd put it, use Option B. It makes no claim about your experience.

### Week 1: "This is what's happening"

#### Day 1. POV: Sunday, 4pm

**On-screen:** POV: Sunday 4pm and the week has already landed on you

**Hook**
> It's Sunday at four in the afternoon, and the week has already landed on you.

**Why**
> Nothing has happened yet. No emails, no meetings. But your shoulders are up and your head is already in Monday.
>
> Recovery researcher Sabine Sonnentag found that switching off from work in your time off is one of four things that restore you. She calls it psychological detachment. The others are relaxing, doing something absorbing that isn't work, and having control over your own time.
>
> On Sunday afternoon you lose detachment, because Monday is unfinished in your head.

**Try this**
> On Friday, before you close the laptop, do two things.
>
> One. Write down the very first task you'll do on Monday. Be specific: "reply to the finance team about the Q3 numbers", not "emails".
>
> Two. Write down anything you're worried you'll forget. Get it out of your head and onto the page.
>
> Then on Sunday, when Monday creeps in, you can tell yourself it's handled. It's written down. You know where to start.

**CTA**
> Try it this Friday, and follow. I'll cover the other three recovery experiences this month.

*Source: Sonnentag and Fritz (2007), Recovery Experience Questionnaire, Journal of Occupational Health Psychology.*

#### Day 2. Stop calling it tired

**On-screen:** Stop calling it tired. Check these 3 things.

**Hook**
> Stop calling it tired.

**Why**
> Tired goes away when you rest. Burnout doesn't, and the World Health Organization describes it with three specific signs.
>
> One. Exhaustion. You run out of energy in a way sleep doesn't fix.
>
> Two. Mental distance from your job. You've gone cynical, or you feel switched off from work you used to care about.
>
> Three. Your sense of being good at your job drops. You second-guess decisions you used to make in five minutes.
>
> The WHO calls burnout an occupational phenomenon. That means it comes from chronic work stress that hasn't been managed. It isn't a personal weakness.

**Try this**
> Score yourself on each sign from zero to ten for the last month.
>
> If one is high, pay attention. If two or three are high, rest alone probably won't fix it, and it's worth getting support. Start with your GP if you're worried.

**CTA**
> Save this and score yourself again in a fortnight. Then compare.

*Source: World Health Organization, ICD-11 definition of burnout (2019).*

#### Day 3. The reliable one

**On-screen:** What nobody tells you about being the reliable one

**Hook**
> Here's what nobody tells you about being the reliable one.

**Why**
> The reward for coping is more to cope with. You deliver, so you get the next thing. And the next.
>
> Because your work never slips, nobody has a reason to ask how you are. My work never slipped, so nobody asked. I didn't ask myself either.
>
> And the reliable person rarely says no, because so much of who they are rests on being the one who copes.

**Try this**
> This week, write down everything you're carrying. Every project, every "quick favour", every thing you picked up because no one else would.
>
> Next to each one, write what would happen if you handed it back. Be honest. Usually it's "someone would be mildly inconvenienced".
>
> Then take the list to your manager and ask one question: "If I can only do four of these well, which four matter most?"
>
> You're not complaining. You're asking for priorities, which is what a good manager wants from you.

**CTA**
> If you're the reliable one, comment "me". I want to know who I'm talking to.

#### Day 4. Quiet cracking

**On-screen:** "Quiet cracking": what it is and what to do

**Hook**
> You've probably seen the phrase "quiet cracking". Here's what's underneath it.

**Why**
> It's a workplace label for people who keep showing up and keep delivering while their energy and morale wear down underneath. It's a social media term, not a diagnosis.
>
> Burnout researchers have a more precise version. The Burnout Assessment Tool, developed by Wilmar Schaufeli and colleagues, measures four core symptoms: exhaustion, mental distance from work, trouble thinking clearly, and trouble managing your emotions.
>
> That last pair matters. Quiet cracking often shows up as forgetting things or snapping at people, long before anyone notices you're tired.

**Try this**
> For one week, keep a note on your phone. Every time you notice one of these four, write it down with the time:
>
> Exhausted. Checked out. Can't think straight. Snapped or teared up.
>
> At the end of the week, look for the pattern. Which one shows up most, and on which days? That tells you where to start.

**CTA**
> Follow, because I'll cover what to do about each one.

*Source: Schaufeli, De Witte and Desart (2020), Burnout Assessment Tool, International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health.*

#### Day 5. Living in the third person

**On-screen:** I watched myself live my own life

**Hook**
> For years I watched myself live my own life.

**Why**
> I was in every meeting. I said the right things. I hit every deadline. And it felt like it was happening to someone else. I was on autopilot.
>
> There are two pieces of research that help explain this.
>
> The first: burnout researchers describe "mental distance". You pull back from your work without ever deciding to.
>
> The second comes from animal studies. A 2009 study found that months of stress weakened the part of the brain that makes deliberate choices, and strengthened the part that runs habits. You keep running the routine without choosing it.

**Try this**
> Pick one small decision each day and make it on purpose. What you eat for lunch. Which route you walk. When you finish.
>
> Before you do it, say to yourself: "I'm choosing this."
>
> It sounds tiny. The point is to practise being the one making the decision again.
>
> And if the feeling of watching yourself from outside is constant or frightening, talk to your GP.

**CTA**
> Follow if you've felt this. You're not the only one.

*Sources: Schaufeli et al. (2020), Burnout Assessment Tool; Dias-Ferreira et al. (2009), Science, a study in rats.*

#### Day 6. Three questions before Monday

**On-screen:** 3 questions before Monday

**Hook**
> Three questions to ask yourself before Monday. Be honest.

**Why**
> Most people only notice burnout when their work slips. If you're good at your job, that's the last thing to go. These three questions look at what goes first.

> One. When did a weekend last leave you feeling rested? Not just less tired. Rested.
>
> Two. Can you switch off at night, or does work come to bed with you?
>
> Three. Are the people you love getting your energy, or what's left of it?

**Try this**
> If you didn't like your answers, look at the three together.

> No to the first means your recovery isn't working. No to the second means you can't detach from work. And "what's left" to the third means your life outside work is paying for it.
>
> I built a free check-in that goes deeper across four areas: your body, your mind, your drive and your life outside work. Fourteen questions, about four minutes. You get a plain-language readout and three small things to try over the next two weeks.

**CTA**
> The link's in my profile. It's a self-reflection tool, not a diagnosis.

#### Day 7. The right to disconnect

**On-screen:** Australia has a right to disconnect. Are you using it?

**Hook**
> Australia has had a right to disconnect since 2024. So why are you still answering at nine at night?

**Why**
> One poll this year found more than a third of working Australians are contacted outside their hours every week. One in five said they can't disconnect at all.
>
> Here's why it matters even when you don't reply. A US study by William Becker and colleagues found that the expectation of checking email after hours was enough to raise employees' anxiety, and the anxiety was linked to worse sleep and lower wellbeing. Their partners felt it too.
>
> So it's not only the time you spend answering. It's the always-on feeling of being on call.

**Try this**
> Tell your team when you'll respond. One line in your email signature or your team channel:
>
> "I read messages between 8am and 6pm. If it's urgent, call me."
>
> Then honour it for two weeks, and notice how few things turn out to be urgent.
>
> This is general information, not legal advice. If you want to know how the law applies to your job, check the Fair Work Ombudsman's website.

**CTA**
> What hours would you put in your line? Tell me in the comments.

*Sources: ADP poll, June 2026 (confirm before filming); Becker, Belkin and Tuskey (2018), Academy of Management Proceedings.*

### Week 2: "Here's why rest isn't working"

#### Day 8. Rest isn't recovery

**On-screen:** You rested all weekend. Why are you still exhausted?

**Hook**
> You rested all weekend. So why are you still exhausted?

**Why**
> Because resting and recovering aren't the same thing.
>
> Rest means you stopped working. Recovery means your body and mind actually came back down. You can lie on the couch for six hours with your head still in the office.
>
> Sabine Sonnentag's research found four experiences that do the recovering:
>
> Detachment: not thinking about work.
> Relaxation: your body slowing down.
> Mastery: doing something absorbing that challenges you, like learning a skill or a hard hike.
> Control: deciding for yourself how you spend the time.
>
> Scrolling on the couch often gives you none of the four.

**Try this**
> Next weekend, plan one thing for each.
>
> Detachment: an hour with your phone in another room.
> Relaxation: a slow walk or a bath.
> Mastery: something you're learning.
> Control: one block of time with no plan, where you decide on the day.
>
> Then on Monday, notice which one did the most for you. That's the one to protect.

**CTA**
> Save this for Saturday.

*Source: Sonnentag and Fritz (2007), Journal of Occupational Health Psychology.*

#### Day 9. Why the holiday wears off

**On-screen:** You took two weeks off. By Wednesday it was gone.

**Hook**
> You took two weeks off. By Wednesday of your first week back, it was gone.

**Why**
> That's not you. That's what the research finds.
>
> Jessica de Bloom and colleagues followed Dutch workers through their holidays. Health and wellbeing went up while they were away, and faded within the first week back at work.
>
> A 2023 meta-analysis of thirteen studies found the same thing. And longer holidays didn't make the benefit last longer.
>
> A holiday lowers the pressure for a while. Then you go back to the same way of working, and it builds again.

**Try this**
> Two changes.
>
> One. Spread your leave out. Short breaks every couple of months instead of saving it all for one long holiday.
>
> Two. Plan your first week back before you leave. No meetings on the first morning. One evening with a hard finish time. And one thing you'll do differently in your normal week.
>
> The holiday can't fix the pattern. Your ordinary Tuesday can.

**CTA**
> How many days did your last holiday last once you were back? Comment the number.

*Sources: de Bloom et al. (2010), Work & Stress; vacation fade-out meta-analysis (2023), European Psychologist.*

#### Day 10. The shutdown routine

**On-screen:** A 5-minute routine to stop work following you home

**Hook**
> If work follows you home every night, you need a way to end the day.

**Why**
> Your brain doesn't know the workday is over unless you tell it. If you close the laptop mid-thought, the thought comes home with you.
>
> In a small sleep-lab study at Baylor University, people who wrote a to-do list for the next day before bed fell asleep faster than people who wrote about what they'd already done. The more specific the list, the faster they fell asleep.

**Try this**
> Five minutes before you finish, every day.
>
> One. Write tomorrow's top three tasks on paper. Specific ones.
> Two. Write anything else that's in your head onto the same page.
> Three. Close every tab and window.
> Four. Say out loud, "That's done for today." It feels silly. Do it anyway.
>
> Then leave the laptop closed.
>
> Give it two weeks. Most people notice their evenings first, before their sleep.

**CTA**
> Save this and set a reminder for five minutes before your finish time.

*Source: Scullin et al. (2018), Baylor University, small study in healthy young adults.*

#### Day 11. Functional freeze

**On-screen:** "Functional freeze": what it is and isn't

**Hook**
> There's a term going around called "functional freeze". Here's what it's pointing at.

**Why**
> People use it for this: you get through the whole workday, and then you get home and can't do anything. Not the dishes. Not the message you owe a friend. You sit on the couch and scroll.
>
> It's a social media label, not a diagnosis.
>
> But the pattern is real. If work takes every bit of energy you have, there's nothing left for the rest of your life. You're not lazy. You're empty.

**Try this**
> Keep something back during the day, not after it.
>
> Take one ten-minute break between meetings with no phone. A review of twenty-two studies found that short breaks of ten minutes or less give a small, real boost to energy and reduce fatigue.
>
> And lower the bar for the evening. Pick one small thing, like a shower and a walk round the block. Do that, and let the rest go.
>
> If the freeze has gone on for weeks, or you've stopped enjoying anything, please see your GP.

**CTA**
> Follow for more ways to keep something back for yourself.

*Source: Albulescu et al. (2022), PLOS ONE, meta-analysis of 22 samples.*

#### Day 12. Stop trying to stay calm

**On-screen:** Stop trying to stay calm under pressure

**Hook**
> Stop trying to stay calm under pressure. That's not what resilient people do.

**Why**
> A 2026 study put sixty people through the same stress test four times over eight weeks. The ones who scored high on resilience didn't react less. Their heart rate went up like everyone else's.
>
> What was different was the recovery. They came back down faster after each round.
>
> It's a small study of young men, so hold it lightly. But it changes the goal. You don't need to feel nothing in the hard meeting. You need to come back down afterwards.

**Try this**
> After your next stressful meeting, don't go straight into the next one.
>
> Take three minutes. Stand up. Walk to the window or around the floor. Breathe out longer than you breathe in, five times.
>
> Then ask yourself one question every evening this week: "After today's hardest moment, how long did it take me to feel normal again?"
>
> Minutes, hours, or not yet. That's your recovery time, and that's the number to shrink.

**CTA**
> What's your number right now? Tell me in the comments.

*Source: Rösner et al. (2026), Comprehensive Psychoneuroendocrinology, 60 young men.*

#### Day 16. The advice you can't take

**On-screen:** You'd know exactly what to tell a colleague

**Hook**
> If a colleague described your week to you, you'd know exactly what to tell them.

**Why**
> Slow down. Hand that project back. Take the leave.
>
> You know the advice. You just can't follow it. That isn't a lack of willpower.
>
> Making a new choice in the moment uses the part of your brain that plans and decides. Months of stress wear that part down, while the routines you already have get easier to run. In animal studies, chronic stress shifted behaviour away from deliberate choices and towards habit.
>
> So when you're depleted, you fall back on the old routine. Even when you know better.

**Try this**
> Shrink your own advice until it fits your worst day.
>
> Write down what you'd tell a colleague. Then make it smaller. Then smaller again.
>
> "Take a holiday" becomes "take Friday afternoon off". Then "finish at five on Friday".
>
> "Stop working evenings" becomes "one evening a week with the laptop closed".
>
> If you could do it on your worst day, it's the right size.

**CTA**
> What would you tell a colleague in your position? Write it in the comments, then make it smaller.

*Source: Dias-Ferreira et al. (2009), Science, a study in rats.*

#### Day 17. Perimenopause and burnout look alike

**On-screen:** Brain fog at 45? It might not be burnout.

**Hook**
> If you're in your forties and your brain has suddenly gone foggy, it might not be burnout.

**Why**
> Perimenopause usually starts in your forties and can last for years. Poor sleep, brain fog and memory slips are common symptoms. They overlap with burnout almost exactly, and they tend to arrive in the same decade you reach your most senior roles.
>
> A Mayo Clinic study of more than four thousand working women aged 45 to 60 found about one in eight reported a negative work outcome from menopause symptoms, like missing work or cutting hours.
>
> If it's hormonal, reducing your workload alone won't fix it.

**Try this**
> Keep a symptom diary for a month. Write down your sleep, your energy, your focus, and your cycle if you have one.
>
> Then take it to your GP and ask directly about perimenopause. The diary makes the conversation much easier.
>
> It can be both, by the way. Burnout and perimenopause can happen at the same time, and each makes the other harder.

**CTA**
> Share this with someone in her forties who keeps saying she's just tired.

*Source: Faubion et al. (2023), Mayo Clinic Proceedings. US data.*

#### Day 18. The 20-minute nature dose

**On-screen:** How long outside actually lowers stress?

**Hook**
> How long do you need to spend outside for it to lower your stress? Researchers measured it.

**Why**
> In a 2019 study, thirty-six city dwellers spent time somewhere outside that felt like nature, a few times a week for eight weeks. Researchers measured the stress hormone cortisol in their saliva before and after.
>
> Cortisol dropped. The best return for the time spent was between twenty and thirty minutes. After that, the benefits kept coming, just more slowly.
>
> They could walk or sit. It didn't have to be exercise.

**Try this**
> Three times this week, spend twenty minutes outside somewhere with trees or water. A park, the harbour, your own street if it's leafy.
>
> No phone, no podcast. The point is to notice where you are.
>
> If twenty minutes feels impossible on a workday, start with ten between meetings. Even short breaks help your energy. Then work up.
>
> It was a small study. But it's free, there's no downside, and you can test it yourself.

**CTA**
> Save this and put three blocks in your calendar now.

*Source: Hunter, Gillespie and Chen (2019), Frontiers in Psychology, 36 participants.*

#### Day 19. Leftovers

**On-screen:** Work gets the best of you. Who gets the rest?

**Hook**
> Work gets the best of you. Who gets what's left?

**Why**
> You're home, you're nodding at the people who matter, and your mind is still at the office.
>
> They notice. In the same study that looked at after-hours email, the researchers found the anxiety didn't stay with the employee. Their partners reported it too.
>
> Being half there isn't neutral for the people around you.

**Try this**
> One evening this week that belongs to someone you care about.
>
> Before it starts, put your phone in another room. Not on silent. Another room.
>
> Tell them what you're doing: "I'm leaving my phone in the study tonight. I want to actually be here."
>
> Then notice how often you reach for it. That's useful information. It shows you how much of your attention work still has.
>
> One evening won't fix everything. It's a start you can repeat.

**CTA**
> Who's your evening for? Tell me below.

*Source: Becker, Belkin and Tuskey (2018), Academy of Management Proceedings.*

#### Day 20. The 10pm reply

**On-screen:** Why you reply at 10pm

**Hook**
> Be honest about the ten o'clock reply. Did it need to go tonight?

**Why**
> An Australian survey this year found that most workers who answer after hours do it to look committed, or to avoid being judged. Not because the work was urgent.
>
> And every reply teaches your team that you're available at ten at night. So they send more at ten at night.
>
> You're paying with your evening to manage an impression.

**Try this**
> Three steps.
>
> One. Write the reply if it helps you get it out of your head. Then use scheduled send for 8am.
>
> Two. For anything truly urgent, agree with your team that they'll call, not email.
>
> Three. Run it for two weeks and keep count. How many times did something actually go wrong because you replied in the morning?
>
> For most people the answer is zero. And that's the evidence you need to keep doing it.

**CTA**
> Follow for more ways to get your evenings back.

*Source: ELMO survey (confirm the 62% figure before filming).*

#### Day 21. Cynicism is a symptom

**On-screen:** You're not becoming a cynical person

**Hook**
> If you've turned cynical about work you used to love, you haven't become a cynical person.

**Why**
> Burnout researchers have treated cynicism as a core sign of burnout for decades. Christina Maslach, who developed the most widely used burnout measure, describes it as pulling away from your work and the people in it.
>
> It works like protection. When you have nothing left, caring less costs less.
>
> So the eye-roll in the team meeting is information. It's telling you how depleted you are.

**Try this**
> Find the part of your job you still care about. There's usually one. A client you like. A kind of problem you're good at. Mentoring someone junior.
>
> This week, protect thirty minutes for it. Put it in your calendar and treat it as non-negotiable.
>
> You're not fixing the cynicism directly. You're reminding yourself why the work used to matter.
>
> If you can't find any part you still care about, that's worth talking to someone about.

**CTA**
> Which part of your job do you still care about? Comment it. Naming it helps.

*Source: Maslach, Schaufeli and Leiter (2001), Annual Review of Psychology.*

### Week 4: "Here's how I help"

#### Day 22. "Am I burnt out enough to get help?"

**On-screen:** "Am I burnt out enough to get help?"

**Hook**
> "Am I burnt out enough to get help?" If you're asking, listen.

**Why**
> Most of the professionals who ask me this haven't collapsed. They're still showing up and still delivering. That's exactly why they doubt it.
>
> But burnout doesn't have a threshold where it suddenly counts. It builds. And the further it goes, the longer the climb back.
>
> The three signs the World Health Organization uses are a good test. Exhaustion that rest doesn't fix. Pulling away from your work. And feeling less capable than you used to.

**Try this**
> Ask yourself three things.
>
> Have I felt like this for more than a month?
> Has rest stopped working?
> Am I telling people I'm fine when I'm not?
>
> If you said yes to two, you don't need to wait for things to get worse. It's easier to start while you still have something in the tank.
>
> If you're struggling to get through the day, or you feel hopeless, see your GP first. That comes before coaching.

*Source: World Health Organization, ICD-11 (2019).*

#### Day 23. The worry window

**On-screen:** Try this: give the worry an appointment

**Hook**
> If one worry follows you around all day, give it an appointment.

**Why**
> This technique is called worry postponement. In the early 1980s, researcher Thomas Borkovec tested it with people who described themselves as worriers. After four weeks, they worried less each day than people who didn't use it.
>
> To be fair to the evidence, later studies have been mixed. It seems to help everyday worriers more than people with an anxiety disorder.
>
> The idea is simple. You're not trying to stop the worry. You're choosing when it gets your attention.

**Try this**
> Pick a fifteen-minute slot later in the day. Same time and place every day. Say, five o'clock at your desk. Not in bed.
>
> When the worry turns up before then, write it down in one line and tell yourself: "Five o'clock."
>
> At five, look at the list. Worry on purpose for fifteen minutes. Some items will have sorted themselves out.
>
> When the time's up, stop.
>
> Try it for two weeks.

**CTA**
> Save this for the next time your mind won't let something go.

*Source: Borkovec et al. (1983), Behaviour Research and Therapy. Later trials mixed.*

#### Day 24. You missed a day. You didn't ruin it.

**On-screen:** Missed a day? You didn't ruin the habit.

**Hook**
> You started a new routine, missed a day, and decided you'd blown it. You hadn't.

**Why**
> You've heard it takes twenty-one days to build a habit. That number came from a plastic surgeon's memoir in 1960. He noticed patients took about three weeks to get used to their new faces. He never studied habits.
>
> When researchers at University College London tracked ninety-six people building a new daily habit, it took anywhere from eighteen days to a projected two hundred and fifty-four. Fewer than half got there in the twelve weeks.
>
> And missing one day barely made a difference to whether the habit formed.

**Try this**
> Pick a habit small enough to do on your worst day. Two minutes of stretching, not a forty-minute workout.
>
> Attach it to something you already do. After I pour my morning coffee, I stretch.
>
> When you miss a day, just do it the next day. No catching up. No starting over.
>
> And give it months, not weeks.

**CTA**
> There's a full write-up on my blog, including why stress makes new habits harder. Link in my profile.

*Source: Lally et al. (2010), European Journal of Social Psychology; covered in the 21-day habit blog post.*

#### Day 25. The caffeine cutoff

**On-screen:** Your 3pm coffee might be costing you an hour of sleep

**Hook**
> Your three o'clock coffee might be costing you an hour of sleep, and you wouldn't know.

**Why**
> In a 2013 study, researchers gave people a strong dose of caffeine at bedtime, three hours before, or six hours before. Even six hours before bed, it cut their sleep by about an hour.
>
> Here's the part that matters. The people in the study didn't notice. Their sleep diaries said they slept fine. The monitors said otherwise.
>
> So if you wake up flat and reach for coffee, then sleep badly, the coffee may be part of the loop.

**Try this**
> Move your caffeine cutoff to midday for two weeks.
>
> Swap your afternoon coffee for decaf, or a walk. Expect the first few days to be harder.
>
> Keep a simple note each morning: how did I sleep, out of ten? At the end of two weeks, compare the first week to the second.
>
> It was a small study, and the dose was high. Your own two weeks will tell you more than I can.

**CTA**
> Save this and start tomorrow.

*Source: Drake et al. (2013), Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine, 12 healthy sleepers.*

#### Day 27. How to decline a meeting

**On-screen:** Copy this line to decline a meeting

**Hook**
> This fortnight, decline one meeting. Here's the exact line to use.

**Why**
> Most professionals sit in meetings they don't need to be in, because saying no feels risky. Especially if you're the reliable one.
>
> But a meeting you're half-present for doesn't help anyone. And every hour in one is an hour you'll make up at night.

**Try this**
> Pick one recurring meeting where you mostly listen. Send this:
>
> "I can't make this one. Could you send me the notes? I'll respond to anything that needs me by Thursday."
>
> That's it. A reason isn't even required. Leave out the three sentences of apology you'll want to add.
>
> If it's your manager's meeting, try: "I'm protecting time for the Q4 report this week. Is it okay if I skip this one and catch up from the notes?"
>
> Then notice what happens. Usually the meeting goes ahead and nobody thinks less of you.

**CTA**
> Save the line. You'll want it for the next invite that lands.

#### Day 28. Tell one person

**On-screen:** The secret that keeps burnout going

**Hook**
> Keeping it secret is part of what keeps burnout going.

**Why**
> When nobody knows, nothing around you changes. People keep handing you more, because you look fine.
>
> My work never slipped, so nobody had a reason to ask. And I didn't tell anyone, because I kept thinking it would ease after this stretch.
>
> Secrecy also means you only have your own view of what's happening, and when you're depleted, your own view isn't the most reliable one.

**Try this**
> Choose one person you trust. A partner, a friend, a colleague who's been through it.
>
> You don't need a big conversation. Try one sentence:
>
> "I'm running closer to the edge than I look, and I wanted someone to know."
>
> Then let them respond. You don't need them to fix it. Saying it out loud changes how real it feels.
>
> If there's no one you can tell, that's important information too, and your GP is a good place to start.

**CTA**
> You don't have to name them. Just decide who your one person is. And follow if this landed.
