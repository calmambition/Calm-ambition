## 1. Decisions before you film anything

### One platform leads. The others get reposts.

Lead with **LinkedIn**. You can only take clients in Australia and New Zealand (insurance), and your buyer there is a mid-to-senior professional in consulting, finance or corporate. About 17 million people in Australia are on LinkedIn, roughly 61% of the population (NapoleonCat ad-audience estimate, June 2026).

The catch: LinkedIn shows your posts to your own network first, and your network sits in Toronto. Until you add Australian and New Zealand connections, your posts will reach Canadians who can't hire you. Section 7 covers how to rebuild the network. Your Toronto contacts still matter as people who can introduce you to Sydney and Auckland colleagues.

Film every video vertical (9:16). Post it natively to LinkedIn first, then upload the same file to TikTok and Instagram Reels. Facebook gets the Reels crosspost and nothing else for now.

Do not write separate content for each platform. At zero followers, three content streams will stall within a month.

### Do not start with "Hi, I'm Priyanka, a burnout coach"

A stranger has no reason to care who you are. They care about the thing they feel on Sunday night. Every video opens on their problem, and your story shows up as proof. Video 1 still introduces you, but it leads with the moment you missed the signs.

### Text posts count

If filming feels like too big a step this week, post the LinkedIn text version of a script first (Section 4 gives each one). Text builds the habit and tests which hooks land before you spend effort on video.

### Rules for every piece

- No em dashes, anywhere. Same rule as the site.
- No claims you cannot back. No client numbers, no "most of my clients", no outcome guarantees. The site already removed unevidenced statistics. Keep it that way.
- You coach. You do not diagnose or treat. If a comment describes something serious, point them to a GP or therapist.
- One call to action per video, and keep it small. The check-in is the default CTA. The discovery call only appears in videos 11 and 12.

## 2. The audience, in their own words

You are talking to one person: still performing, still the dependable one, privately running on empty. The site phrases that land hardest, use them as hooks:

- "Weekends don't restore anything anymore."
- "Fine, just tired."
- "Two weeks away changed nothing."
- "I can see exactly what I'd tell a colleague. I just can't do it myself."
- "I think this is just who I am now."

Four content pillars, matching the check-in (Body, Mind, Drive, Life), plus a fifth for you:

| Pillar | What it covers | Example angle |
|---|---|---|
| Body | Sleep, energy, tension, caffeine | Why you wake up tired after eight hours |
| Mind | Switching off, looping thoughts, focus | The meeting you replay at 11pm |
| Drive | Cynicism, flatness, self-doubt | When the work you were proud of goes grey |
| Life | Cancelled plans, leftovers for family | Giving the people you love what's left |
| Story | Your corporate years, the stop, Australia, modelling | Getting through meetings without being in them |

## 3. The first 12 videos, in order

Each runs 30 to 60 seconds, about 80 to 150 words spoken. Film on your phone, eye level, natural light from a window in front of you. One take per section is fine. Captions on every video (CapCut or the platform's auto-captions, then fix errors).

The order builds trust on purpose: recognition first (videos 1 to 4), then explanation (5 to 8), then you as a person (9 and 10), then the invitation (11 and 12). Post two a week, so this covers six weeks.

---

### Video 1. "I spent 15 years at KPMG, Deloitte and CIBC. I missed the signs."

**Goal:** introduce yourself through the problem.
**On-screen text (first 2 seconds):** I was burning out and nobody noticed. Including me.

**Script:**

> I spent fifteen years in corporate. KPMG, Deloitte, CIBC, and an MBA part-time on top.
>
> My work never slipped. That's why nobody asked if I was okay. I didn't ask myself either.
>
> By the end I was getting through meetings without being in them. Saying the right things, remembering none of it afterwards.
>
> I kept telling myself it would ease once this stretch was over. There was always another stretch.
>
> I'm Priyanka. I live in Sydney now, and I'm a burnout recovery coach. I work with people across Australia and New Zealand who are where I was: still delivering, with nothing left underneath.
>
> If that's you, follow along. I'm going to talk about the parts nobody mentions.

**CTA:** follow.
**Pin this video** to your TikTok and Instagram profile and feature it on LinkedIn.

### Video 2. "Weekends don't restore you anymore."

**Goal:** recognition. The single most relatable symptom.
**On-screen text:** If Saturday doesn't fix it, this is why.

**Script:**

> If you get to Sunday afternoon and feel the week landing on you before it's even started, listen.
>
> You're built to rise to a demand and then come back down. Pressure arrives, you perform, the pressure passes, your body gets the all-clear.
>
> Burnout starts when that all-clear stops arriving. The work keeps coming, so your system never comes fully back down.
>
> That's why the weekend doesn't work. You stop working, but your body is still running the alarm. You're lying still. You're not recovering.
>
> Rest and recovery are different things. Next video, I'll show you the difference.

**CTA:** follow for part two.
**Visual:** if you can, screen-record the five-rounds chart on the Burnout Explained section of the site and show it behind you.

---

### Video 3. "Two weeks off changed nothing."

**Goal:** name a frustration people feel stupid admitting.
**On-screen text:** You took the holiday. You came back just as tired.

**Script:**

> You finally took the two weeks. You came back, and by Wednesday it was like you'd never left.
>
> That doesn't mean you're broken. It means the holiday was treating the wrong thing.
>
> When you've been running hot for months, the floor you start from keeps climbing. Two weeks off lowers it a little. Then you walk straight back into the same way of working, and it climbs again.
>
> For me, the problem was never the workload. It was how I'd been running the whole time.
>
> A holiday is a pause. What changes it is how you work on an ordinary Tuesday.

**CTA:** comment "Tuesday" if your last holiday wore off in a week. (Comments early on help reach.)

---

### Video 4. "'Fine, just tired.'"

**Goal:** the identity hook. People will share this one.
**On-screen text:** How often have you said "fine, just tired" this month?

**Script:**

> "How are you?" "Fine. Just tired."
>
> I said that for years. It's the most efficient lie in corporate life, because it's half true and nobody follows up.
>
> Here's what "just tired" was covering for me. I couldn't switch off at night. Things I used to enjoy went flat. I was cancelling on people and calling it being busy.
>
> Tired goes away when you sleep. If yours doesn't, it might be something else.
>
> You don't have to tell everyone. But it's worth telling yourself.

**CTA:** "I've built a free four-minute check-in that looks at your body, mind, drive and life. Link in my profile."

### Video 5. "Why high performers are the last to notice."

**Goal:** speak to the audience's self-image. Draws on the existing blog post.
**On-screen text:** Being good at your job hides burnout.

**Script:**

> The people who burn out hardest are often the ones nobody worries about.
>
> If your work is still good, you have no evidence anything is wrong. Your manager doesn't see it. Your team doesn't see it. You don't either, because you're measuring yourself by output, and output is the last thing to go.
>
> Everything else goes first. Sleep. Patience. The evenings. The people at home.
>
> So if you're waiting for your performance to slip before you take this seriously, you'll be waiting until it's expensive.

**CTA:** "I wrote more on this. Blog link in my profile." Links to `blog-why-high-performers.html`.

---

### Video 6. "Cortisol isn't the enemy."

**Goal:** authority. Correct a myth with a clear explanation.
**On-screen text:** Stop trying to "lower your cortisol."

**Script:**

> Every wellness account wants you to lower your cortisol. That's the wrong goal.
>
> Cortisol is what gets you out of bed and sharp for a nine o'clock presentation. You need it.
>
> The problem is rhythm. Cortisol should be high in the morning and fall across the day, so you can wind down at night. Under chronic stress that rhythm flattens. You drag in the morning and you're wired at eleven at night.
>
> So the goal isn't less stress hormone. It's getting the rhythm back. Anchoring your wake time every day, weekends included, is one of the simplest places to start.

**CTA:** blog link to `blog-cortisol-not-the-enemy.html`.
**Before posting:** reread the blog post and make sure every claim here matches it. You corrected a study claim there once already.

---

### Video 7. "The 21-day habit rule isn't real."

**Goal:** myth-busting that relieves guilt.
**On-screen text:** If your new routine fell apart on day 9, read this.

**Script:**

> You've heard it takes 21 days to build a habit. That number didn't come from habit research.
>
> When researchers actually tracked people building new habits, it took a lot longer, and it varied wildly from person to person.
>
> Why this matters if you're burnt out: you try a new routine, it falls apart in week two, and you decide you're the problem.
>
> You're not. You were working to a deadline that never existed.
>
> Pick something small enough that you'd do it on your worst day, and give it far longer than three weeks.

**CTA:** blog link to `blog-habit-formation-myth.html`.
**Before posting:** check the figures you quote against the blog post's sources. Keep the spoken version vague ("a lot longer") unless you're certain of the number.

---

### Video 8. "One thing to try tonight."

**Goal:** give value with no strings. Practical videos get saved.
**On-screen text:** If your brain won't switch off at night, try this.

**Script:**

> If you lie in bed running through tomorrow, try this tonight.
>
> Before you close the laptop, write tomorrow's top three things on paper. Paper, not your phone.
>
> Then everything else stays on the page. Your brain keeps looping because it's scared you'll forget. Writing it down gives it somewhere to put it.
>
> It won't fix burnout. It's one small hard stop at the end of the day, and most people I talk to have none.
>
> Try it for two weeks and notice what your evenings do.

**CTA:** "Save this for tonight."
**Note:** this matches intervention 5 in the check-in library, so the advice stays consistent with what the readout gives people.

---

### Video 9. "I moved to the other side of the world. You probably can't."

**Goal:** your full story, and why your method fits their life.
**On-screen text:** I fixed my burnout by stopping everything. That's the problem.

**Script:**

> When I burnt out, I left Toronto and moved to Australia. Two weeks after I landed, the world went into lockdown.
>
> No job. No plan. Nothing waiting. For the first time in my adult life, I had a complete stop.
>
> That's how I learned what my capacity actually was, and where my boundaries had to sit.
>
> But here's the honest part. You probably can't do that. You have people relying on you and work that won't pause.
>
> So your version has to happen inside the life you already have. That's harder than what I did, and it's the only thing I work on now.

**CTA:** follow.

---

### Video 10. "Is coaching therapy? I needed both."

**Goal:** answer the biggest objection and build trust by drawing the line yourself.
**On-screen text:** Coaching or therapy? Here's how I'd decide.

**Script:**

> People ask me if burnout coaching is just therapy with a different name. It's not, and I know because I needed both.
>
> Work pushed me into therapy. What came up there went a long way past work, and that took years.
>
> Coaching does a different job. It's about working out your capacity and where your boundaries have to sit so you can keep a demanding career without it costing you everything else.
>
> I don't diagnose or treat anything. If what someone tells me needs a therapist, I say so.
>
> If you're not sure which you need, start by talking to your GP.

**CTA:** none. Let this one build trust.

---

### Video 11. "What the first conversation looks like."

**Goal:** lower the fear of booking. First direct mention of the call.
**On-screen text:** What actually happens on a discovery call.

**Script:**

> If you've thought about getting help with burnout but the idea of a sales call makes you want to close the tab, this is for you.
>
> My discovery call is thirty minutes. I ask what's draining you. Most people know, and it's usually something they've been working around for years without naming.
>
> We talk about what's going on and whether my programme is the right fit. Sometimes it isn't, and I'll tell you.
>
> There's no pitch. People usually leave with a clearer picture of what's been going on, whatever they decide.

**CTA:** "Link in my profile if you want to book one."

### Video 12. "Take the check-in."

**Goal:** convert viewers into leads.
**On-screen text:** Running on empty? Find out in four minutes.

**Script:**

## 4. LinkedIn text versions

LinkedIn rewards text posts with a strong first two lines (the part before "see more"). For each video, post the transcript as text with the first line as a hook and short paragraphs. Example for Video 2:

> Weekends don't restore anything anymore.
>
> If that's you, you're not lazy and you're not imagining it.
>
> You're built to rise to a demand and come back down once it passes. Burnout starts when the "it's passed" signal stops arriving.
>
> So you stop working on Saturday, but your body is still running the alarm. You're lying still. You're not recovering.
>
> Rest and recovery are different things, and most high performers only get the first.
>
> What's the first sign you notice when you're running too hot?

End text posts with a question. Do not put links in the post body (put them in the first comment), as many LinkedIn users report link posts get less reach. Treat that as a common belief rather than a confirmed rule, and test it yourself.

## 7. Building a network in Australia and New Zealand from scratch

You can't sell content to an audience that doesn't exist yet. For the first 90 days, split your time roughly like this:

**About half: in person and one to one.**
- **Referral partners.** HR and People & Culture leads, psychologists, GPs, career coaches and EAP providers all see burnt-out professionals every week. Build a list of 20, meet five for coffee, and agree how you refer to each other. Five people who send referrals beat 5,000 followers.
- **Free talks.** Offer a 30-minute talk on rest versus recovery to firm wellbeing committees, women's networks and professional bodies. You get a room of your exact buyer and permission to follow up. Your Big 4 background gets you in the door.
- **Alumni routes.** KPMG Australia runs an alumni program with over 10,000 members and events nationally. Email them and ask whether a KPMG Canada alum can join. Rotman lists no Sydney chapter and invites alumni to start one, which is another way to get known.

**About a third: LinkedIn aimed at AU and NZ.**
- Connect the same day with everyone you meet, with a short note.
- Comment daily on Australian and New Zealand voices (section 6).
- Post two to three times a week from this plan. Text first while reach is small.

**The rest: short video as a crosspost only.** Don't spend extra time on TikTok or Facebook until LinkedIn brings in conversations.

### The hook patterns, turned into your versions

1. **"[Common fix] doesn't fix burnout."** (Kermiet)
   Yours: "A holiday doesn't fix burnout." "A new job doesn't fix burnout." "Sleeping in on Saturday doesn't fix burnout."
2. **"The [number]-question test."** (Kermiet)
   Yours: "Three questions to ask yourself before Monday." This one leads straight to your check-in, so it may be your best converting format.
3. **"Things I needed to hear when..."** (Kermiet)
   Yours: "Things I needed to hear in my last year at Deloitte."
4. **A named rule.** (Nerurkar's Rule of Two)
   Yours: name one of your own. For example, "the Tuesday test": if the change doesn't survive an ordinary Tuesday, it isn't a change. Only name it if you will keep using it.
5. **A picture in numbers.** (Nerurkar's room of 30)
   Yours: only with a sourced statistic, and link the source in the caption. The site already removed unsourced figures. Keep that standard on social.
6. **Credential plus confession.** (Gisèle)
   Yours: Video 1 already does this. Reuse the shape: "I was a [credential]. Here's what nobody in that building said out loud."

### Where copying goes wrong

- **Their current posts work because they already have an audience.** A 75K account can post a vague thought and get reach. You can't yet. Scroll back to their first 30 to 50 posts and study those.
- **Copy structure, never wording.** Take the hook shape and the length, then write it from your own story. Their followers will notice a lift, and so will you every time you post it.
- **Most of the visible burnout content online is quit-your-job content.** Your angle, staying in the career and changing how you run it, is less crowded. Don't trade it for a format that's working for someone else's message.

### 30-minute teardown you do yourself

Search can't see view counts. The apps can. Once, before you film batch one:

1. On TikTok, open each account, tap Popular (or sort by most viewed). Write down their top 10 videos.
2. For each one, note: the first sentence spoken, the on-screen text, the length, and whether they face the camera or use B-roll.
3. On LinkedIn, search "burnout" and filter Posts by the past month. Note the first two lines of the five posts with the most comments.
4. Mark which hooks fit one of the six patterns above. Anything that doesn't fit is a new pattern. Add it here.

## 10. What to measure (and what to ignore)

Ignore follower counts for the first three months.

Track these weekly in a spreadsheet:

- Check-in completions (GA4 events already exist on the check-in page)
- Discovery calls booked
- DMs from people describing their situation
- Which hooks got the most comments or saves

After video 12, keep the three best-performing hooks and make five more videos in the same shape. Drop the pillar that got the least response.
