## 0. The strategy in plain words

**The goal.** Book discovery calls with professionals in Australia and New Zealand. Followers, views and likes only count if they lead there.

**Who it's for.** One person: a mid-career professional, ideally in accounting, consulting or banking, still performing well and privately running on empty. Your credibility is highest with the industry you came from (see section 9 for why).

**The path a stranger takes to becoming a client.**

1. They see a post that describes their week better than they could.
2. They tap your profile and see the same message: Big 4 background, Sydney, burnout recovery.
3. A few posts later they take the check-in.
4. They get a readout by email that names their pattern.
5. They book a discovery call, or come back to it weeks later.

Every post does one job on that path. Story and Recognise posts handle step 1. Your profile handles step 2. Invite posts handle steps 3 and 5.

**Why it takes months.** People in burnout don't book a call off one post. They watch you for weeks first. You're building familiarity, so the most important number in the first three months is consistency: did you post three times a week?

**Where your time goes.**

| Track | Share of your time | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| In person: referral partners and free talks | About half | Fastest route to first clients in a city where nobody knows you |
| LinkedIn: posting and commenting on AU/NZ voices | About a third | Builds the familiarity that makes people book later |
| Guest articles in AU trade press | One pitch a month | Borrowed credibility and an audience you don't have yet (section 9) |
| Short video crossposts | Whatever's left | Discovery by strangers. A bonus while you start, not a priority. |

**How you'll know it's working.** By the end of January: at least five introductions from your network, check-ins arriving from LinkedIn, and at least one discovery call that started from a post. If posts get comments but nobody takes the check-in, the Invite posts need work. If nobody comments, the hooks need work.

### Why the first post is text and your story

Your first posts go to your existing network first, and that network is in Toronto. Personal news ("here's what I do now, and I'm in Sydney") is the post type your network is most likely to react to. Their reactions push the post further. And the ask at the end turns Toronto contacts into introducers, because they can't be your clients.

### Why the first video is problem-led

Video goes further than your network, to strangers. A stranger doesn't care who you are yet. They care about the thing they feel on Sunday night. So the video opens on the problem and your story proves you've lived it.

### Why two text posts for each video

- You can write a text post in 20 minutes. A video takes filming, captions and editing.
- At zero followers you need to find out which hooks land. Text tests hooks cheaply. The best text posts become future videos.
- LinkedIn is where your buyers are, and text posts work well there.

## 2. How the content is structured

### Five post types

Every post is one of these. The schedule rotates them so nobody sees three of the same in a row.

| Type | Job | Share of posts |
|---|---|---|
| **Story** | Your corporate years, the stop, moving to Sydney. Builds trust. | About 1 in 5 |
| **Recognise** | Names a symptom so precisely the reader feels seen. Gets comments. | About 1 in 3 |
| **Explain** | Why it happens: rest vs recovery, cortisol, habits. Builds authority. | About 1 in 5 |
| **Try this** | One small, specific thing to do. Gets saves. | About 1 in 6 |
| **Invite** | Check-in or discovery call. Converts. | About 1 in 7 |

### Four themes (same as the check-in)

Body (sleep, energy, tension), Mind (switching off, looping thoughts), Drive (cynicism, flatness, doubt), Life (cancelled plans, family getting leftovers).

### The arc of the first 20

1. **Posts 1 to 6: "This is me, and this is you."** Story and recognition. No selling.
2. **Posts 7 to 12: "Here's why it happens."** Explanation and one practical tip. First check-in mention at post 10.
3. **Posts 13 to 15: widen the circle.** A post for managers (your referrers), the move to Sydney, a summer break post.
4. **Posts 16 to 20: "Here's how I help."** New year, the therapy question, the discovery call, the method, the check-in.

### Rules for every post

- No em dashes. Same rule as the site.
- No statistic without a source you can link. No client numbers, no "most of my clients", no outcome promises.
- You coach. You don't diagnose or treat. If a comment describes something serious, point them to a GP or Lifeline (13 11 14 in Australia).
- Never claim the check-in stores nothing. It sends the readout to you through Formspree (see the note at the end).
- Links go in the first comment, not the post body. Many LinkedIn users believe link posts get less reach. Treat that as unproven and test it.
- No hashtags on LinkedIn. Three on TikTok and Instagram: `#burnout #burnoutrecovery` plus `#sydney` or `#corporatelife`.

Every first-person line in a post must come from here. If a script says something about you that isn't on the list, cut it or confirm it first. Add to the list as you share more.

### How to use the scripts

They're word for word so you never stare at a blank page. Don't read them off a screen on camera, because it shows. Read the script aloud three times, then film it in sections, one paragraph per take, looking at the lens. Change any word that doesn't sound like you. The scripts are yours to edit; the structure (hook, middle, one CTA) is the part to keep.

Filming setup: phone at eye level, window light in front of you, quiet room, vertical. Captions on every video (CapCut auto-captions, then fix errors).

### Post 4. Monday 23 November. LinkedIn text. Explain.

> There's a stage of burnout that doesn't feel like stress.
>
> It feels like autopilot.
>
> In my last years in corporate I was still doing all of it. The meetings, the deadlines, being the dependable one. But I was living my life in the third person, watching myself do it, as if it were happening to someone else.
>
> Burnout research has words for two parts of this.
>
> The first is "mental distance". You pull back from your work without deciding to. You keep turning up, but you've stopped being in it.
>
> The second is about habit. In animal studies, months of stress weakened the part of the brain that makes deliberate choices and strengthened the part that runs routines. You keep doing what you've always done, without choosing it.
>
> That's autopilot. It's convincing, because from the outside it looks exactly like coping.
>
> If you've felt like a passenger in your own week, you're not imagining it. It's one of the quietest signs, and one of the easiest to miss.
>
> If the feeling of watching yourself from the outside is constant or frightening, raise it with your GP.

**First comment:**

> The habit research, explained properly: https://calmambition.github.io/Calm-ambition/blog-habit-formation-myth.html

**Where the science comes from:**
- "Mental distance" is one of four core symptoms in the Burnout Assessment Tool (Schaufeli, De Witte and Desart, 2020), alongside exhaustion and problems with thinking and emotions. It means pulling back from work psychologically.
- The habit finding is the 2009 *Science* study by Dias-Ferreira's team on rats, already cited in your 21-day habit blog post. Keep "in animal studies" in the post, because the study used rats.
- **Don't call this "depersonalisation".** In Maslach's burnout model that word means going cold towards the people you work with, which isn't what you mean. In psychiatry it names a clinical condition. "Autopilot" and "living in the third person" say it more accurately and don't sound like a diagnosis.

### Post 11. Wednesday 9 December. Video. Explain.

**On-screen text:** Stop trying to "lower your cortisol."

**Script:**

> Every wellness account wants you to lower your cortisol. That's the wrong goal.
>
> Cortisol is what gets you out of bed and sharp for a nine o'clock presentation. You need it.
>
> The problem in burnout is timing. Cortisol should spike in the morning and fall across the day, so you can wind down at night. Under months of stress that rhythm breaks. The morning spike flattens, so you wake up flat. And it stays up into the evening, so you're wired at eleven at night.
>
> So the goal isn't less cortisol. It's getting the rhythm back. One of the simplest places to start is the same wake time every day, weekends included.

### From the briefings: use, wait, or skip

- **Use later (idea 34, "given more work because you're good"):** Melody Wilding in HBR, 16 September 2026. Leaders who absorb their team's overflow delay a structural conversation instead of fixing it. It's expert opinion built on one case, so present it as a view, not a finding.
- **Use later, with care (new idea):** perimenopause and work. A Mayo Clinic study of 4,440 working women aged 45 to 60 found about 13% reported an adverse work outcome from symptoms, and estimated US$1.8 billion a year in lost workdays (Faubion et al., *Mayo Clinic Proceedings*, 2023). It's US data. Brain fog and poor sleep overlap with burnout, so tell people to see their GP; don't coach it as burnout. The briefing's "40% considered leaving" figure isn't verified, so leave it out.
- **Wait:** "Burnout gets worse with seniority" (Sarilho et al., 7 November). One niche study in Brazil, no sample size in the coverage. Too thin for a post.
- **Skip for now:** the nature meta-analysis effect sizes (most trials compared nature against doing nothing, which inflates the effect), and resting HRV posts (your audience doesn't need wearable debates yet).
